Try to place a 5G NR carrier exactly at 3500 MHz. It sounds like the most natural choice in band n78. It is also impossible.

Convert 3500 MHz to an NR-ARFCN and you get 633333.33. Channel numbers are integers, so 3500 MHz is not a channel. The two closest frequency centers are 3499.995 MHz and 3500.010 MHz.

This post explains why, and gives you the arithmetic to check any frequency yourself.

## A raster is a grid of allowed frequency centers

To answer the 3500 MHz question, we first need to know what "allowed" means.

A transmitter cannot pick any center frequency it likes. It must pick one from a predefined grid. The spacing of that grid is the **frequency raster**.

With a 100 kHz raster, 1940.10 MHz, 1940.20 MHz, 1940.30 MHz, and 1940.40 MHz are valid frequency centers. 1940.15 MHz is not.

### LTE

LTE uses a single 100 kHz raster, called the **channel raster**. The synchronization signals and the broadcast channel always sit at the center of the LTE carrier. Find the carrier center and you have found the synchronization signals too.

That one-to-one link is convenient, but it ties two different needs together.

### NR

NR breaks that link and defines two rasters:

- The **channel raster** says where the carrier center can be. It has fine resolution, so operators can place a carrier flexibly inside their spectrum.
- The **synchronization raster** says where the SS/PBCH Block can be. It has coarse resolution, so a UE scanning a band checks far fewer positions and finishes the scan faster.

In this post we will cover only the channel raster.

## The global raster and the NR-ARFCN

To say "3500 MHz is not a channel", we need a numbering scheme that covers every possible channel.

3GPP defines one **global frequency raster** from 0 to 100 GHz. Every point on it has a number, the NR-ARFCN. Every band's channel raster is built on top of it.

The spacing of the global raster ($\Delta F_\text{Global}$) grows with frequency. If 5 kHz steps were used all the way to 100 GHz, the channel numbers would reach about 20 million. Three sections keep the numbers smaller (Table 1).

| Frequency Range ($F_{\text{REF}}$) | $\Delta F_{\text{Global}}$ | $F_{\text{REF-OFFS}}$ (MHz) | $N_{\text{REF-OFFS}}$ | NR-ARFCN Range |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 0 — 3 GHz | 5 kHz | 0 | 0 | 0 — 599000 |
| 3 — 24.25 GHz | 15 kHz | 3000 | 600000 | 600000 — 2016666 |
| 24.25 — 100 GHz | 60 kHz | 24250.08 | 2016667 | 2016667 — 3279165 |

Per 3GPP TS 38.104, the relationship between the frequency and the NR-ARFCN is defined by:

$$
F_\text{REF} = F_\text{REF-Offs} + \Delta F_\text{Global}\,(N_\text{REF} - N_\text{REF-Offs})
$$

Or, solving for N_REF (the NR-ARFCN):

$$
N_\text{REF} = N_\text{REF-Offs} + \frac{F_\text{REF} - F_\text{REF-Offs}}{\Delta F_\text{Global}}
$$

Let's do 3500 MHz by hand. It falls in the second section:

N_REF = 600000 + (3500 − 3000) / 0.015 = 600000 + 33333.33 = 633333.33

That is not an integer, so 3500 MHz is not on the global raster. The same arithmetic works for any frequency: pick the section from Table 1 and apply the equation.

Being on the global raster is necessary but not sufficient. Next we check what a specific band allows.

## The band's channel raster

A carrier lives in a band, so we need to know which global raster points that band accepts.

Each band has a **channel raster** with spacing $\Delta F_\text{Raster}$. It uses either every point of the global raster or a subset of it. The step between valid NR-ARFCNs is:

$$
\text{Step} = \frac{\Delta F_\text{Raster}}{\Delta F_\text{Global}}
$$

For example, band n1 uses a 100 kHz raster on a 5 kHz global raster, so only every 20th NR-ARFCN is valid. Band n78 offers two rasters on a 15 kHz global raster: 15 kHz (step 1) and 30 kHz (step 2).

A frequency is valid for a band and raster when three conditions hold:

1. Its NR-ARFCN is an integer.
2. The NR-ARFCN lies between First and Last.
3. (N_REF − First) is a multiple of Step.

We can now validate any carrier. The remaining question is why the bands use such different rasters.

## Why 100 kHz in some bands and 15/30 kHz in others?

Two families of channel raster exist in Frequency Range 1 (FR1):

- **100 kHz raster**. Most bands below 3 GHz use it. LTE also uses 100 kHz, so NR and LTE share the same set of possible frequency centers, which improves coexistence between the two.
- **Subcarrier-based rasters (15 and 30 kHz)**. Here the raster spacing matches the subcarrier spacing.

## Back to 3500 MHz

We opened this post with a carrier at exactly 3500 MHz. Here is the full answer.

- 3500 MHz is NR-ARFCN 633333.33, so it is not on the global raster and no band can use it.
- 3499.995 MHz (NR-ARFCN 633333) is valid in n78, but only on the 15 kHz raster.
- 3500.010 MHz (NR-ARFCN 633334) is valid on both the 15 kHz and 30 kHz rasters of n78.

If you want "3500 MHz" in n78, configure NR-ARFCN 633334 and accept a 10 kHz offset.
