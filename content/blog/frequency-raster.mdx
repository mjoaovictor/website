Try to configure a 5G NR carrier exactly at 3500 MHz. It sounds like the most natural choice in band n78. But a transmitter cannot be configured at that frequency.

Convert 3500 MHz to an NR-ARFCN and you get 633333.33.

NR-ARFCNs are integers, so this frequency does not lie on the global frequency raster.

The two closest valid frequencies are 3499.995 MHz and 3500.010 MHz.

Why does this happen? And how can you check whether any frequency is valid for a given NR band?

Let's work through the details.

## A frequency raster is a grid of allowed frequencies

A transmitter cannot use an arbitrary frequency. It must follow a predefined frequency grid, known as a **frequency raster**.

For example, with a 100 kHz raster, valid frequencies include 1940.10 MHz, 1940.20 MHz, 1940.30 MHz, and 1940.40 MHz. A point such as 1940.15 MHz does not belong to that grid.

LTE and NR define their frequency rasters differently.

### LTE

LTE uses a 100 kHz raster.

Its synchronization signals and broadcast channel sit at the center of the LTE carrier. Find the carrier center and you have found them too.

That link is convenient, but it ties two different needs together.

### NR

NR defines two distinct frequency rasters for different purposes.

- **Channel Raster:** defines where the carrier center can be. It has fine resolution, so operators can place a carrier flexibly inside their spectrum.
- **Synchronization Raster:** defines where the SS/PBCH Block can be, helping a UE search for synchronization signals without scanning every possible carrier frequency.

For the rest of this post, we will focus on the channel raster.

## The global frequency raster and NR-ARFCN

[3GPP TS 38.104](https://portal.3gpp.org/desktopmodules/Specifications/SpecificationDetails.aspx?specificationId=3202) defines a global frequency raster covering frequencies from 0 to 100 GHz.

Each frequency on this raster is identified by an integer called the **NR Absolute Radio Frequency Channel Number (NR-ARFCN)**.

The global frequency raster uses three sections, each with a different frequency step.

| Frequency Range ($F_{\text{REF}}$) | $\Delta F_{\text{Global}}$ | $F_{\text{REF-OFFS}}$ (MHz) | $N_{\text{REF-OFFS}}$ | NR-ARFCN Range |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 0 – 3 GHz | 5 kHz | 0 | 0 | 0 – 599999 |
| 3 – 24.25 GHz | 15 kHz | 3000 | 600000 | 600000 – 2016666 |
| 24.25 – 100 GHz | 60 kHz | 24250.08 | 2016667 | 2016667 – 3279165 |

The frequency ($F_\text{REF}$) is calculated as:

$$
F_\text{REF} = F_\text{REF-Offs} + \Delta F_\text{Global}\,(N_\text{REF} - N_\text{REF-Offs})
$$

Or, solving for $N_\text{REF}$ (the NR-ARFCN):

$$
N_\text{REF} = N_\text{REF-Offs} + \frac{F_\text{REF} - F_\text{REF-Offs}}{\Delta F_\text{Global}}
$$

### Let's calculate the NR-ARFCN for 3500 MHz

3500 MHz falls in the 3 – 24.25 GHz section.

$$
N_\text{REF} = 600000 + \frac{3500 - 3000}{0.015} = 600000 + 33333.33 = 633333.33
$$

The result is not an integer.

Therefore, 3500 MHz is not a valid frequency on the global frequency raster.

This is the first test. But it is not the last.

## The band's channel raster

A frequency must also comply with the channel raster of the specific NR operating band.

Each band defines its permitted NR-ARFCN range and the applicable channel raster spacing.

The relationship between the band's raster and the global raster is:

$$
\text{Step} = \frac{\Delta F_\text{Raster}}{\Delta F_\text{Global}}
$$

For example, band n1 uses a 100 kHz raster on a 5 kHz global raster, so only every 20th NR-ARFCN is valid.

To validate an NR-ARFCN for a specific band and raster, check three conditions:

1. The NR-ARFCN is an integer.
2. It lies between the band's defined First and Last NR-ARFCN.
3. The difference between the NR-ARFCN and First is an integer multiple of the applicable Step.

In other words, a frequency can lie on the global raster and still be invalid for a particular band or raster.

## Back to 3500 MHz

Now we can answer the original question.

3500 MHz corresponds to NR-ARFCN 633333.33, so it is not on the global frequency raster.

3499.995 MHz corresponds to NR-ARFCN 633333. It is valid in n78, but only on the 15 kHz channel raster.

3500.010 MHz corresponds to NR-ARFCN 633334. It is valid in n78 on both the 15 kHz and 30 kHz channel rasters.

If your target is as close as possible to 3500 MHz while complying with the n78 channel raster, NR-ARFCN 633334 is the nearest valid option. Its frequency is 10 kHz above the target.

One important distinction: the NR-ARFCN identifies a frequency reference point. The actual geometric center of a configured carrier also depends on the carrier's resource block configuration and the applicable frequency-mapping rules.
